Living in Palo Alto: Compare Neighborhoods and Full Daily Routes

Palo Alto runs from the Baylands to the foothills, and the experience changes a lot along the way. The city name alone will not settle school placement or commute, so each home has to be checked by address.

Two Caltrain stations, Stanford next door, older neighborhoods and Eichlers, and the Baylands at the edge of town

This is a concise English edition. The full guide in Chinese includes maps, more detail and a half-day tour route.

City overview

Palo Alto sits at the southern end of the San Francisco Peninsula and is the northernmost city in Santa Clara County. It is shaped like a long strip running from the bay into the hills: the Baylands and US-101 at the northeast end, homes around the Caltrain tracks, Alma Street and El Camino Real in the middle, and Palo Alto Hills and the foothill preserves past Foothill Expressway and I-280.

Many homes are older, and rail crossings, flood zones and flight paths all need to be checked by address. An address that says Palo Alto may not line up with city services, school district and neighborhood name at the same time: the Stanford campus is unincorporated county land, East Palo Alto is a separate city, and PAUSD notes that some Palo Alto addresses fall outside the district. The neighborhood names below are common usage, not legal boundaries or school attendance areas.

Neighborhoods

These names are common usage, not legal boundaries.

Downtown / University Ave

Centered on University Avenue and the Palo Alto Caltrain station, this area runs from San Francisquito Creek to around Embarcadero Road and takes in Professorville and Crescent Park. Housing is the most mixed in the city, from apartments and small multifamily buildings to early-1900s wood-frame homes in the Professorville Historic District, where exterior changes can face historic review, and large-lot houses in Crescent Park. You can walk to Whole Foods, the library and the train, which also serves Express trains, but expect permit parking, creek flood exposure and train horns at the Palo Alto Avenue crossing.

Old Palo Alto

Generally the residential area between Embarcadero Road and Oregon Expressway, from Alma Street to Middlefield Road. It is almost entirely single-family homes on wide, tree-lined streets, with lots that are usually larger than in the south and a mix of early houses, postwar homes and recent rebuilds. There is little commerce inside the neighborhood, so daily trips head to Midtown, California Avenue or Town & Country Village; homes on the Alma side live with the rail line, and homes near Oregon or Embarcadero need a traffic-noise check.

California Ave / College Terrace / Evergreen Park

This is the old town of Mayfield, built around the California Avenue shopping street and Caltrain station, with Evergreen Park and Southgate north of the street, College Terrace beside Stanford and Stanford Research Park, and the changing Ventura area to the south. Small-lot older homes sit on narrow streets, with apartments and townhomes near California Avenue and El Camino Real, and the North Ventura plan aims to turn the former Fry's site into a mixed-use district. Walking and biking cover most errands, but expect permit parking, heavy traffic at Page Mill and El Camino, and construction ahead.

Midtown / Mitchell Park

A broad residential area south of Oregon Expressway, between the tracks and US-101, with neighborhood shops along Middlefield Road and Mitchell Park at its southern end. Most homes are 1950s and 1960s single-story houses. It is central but neither station is close, and much of the eastern side is in the FEMA AE flood zone, which affects insurance and rebuilding.

Barron Park

West of El Camino Real and south of Stanford Research Park, this neighborhood has Barron and Matadero creeks running through it and irregular streets, many without sidewalks or curbs. Homes vary widely in age and size, so lot lines, easements, permit history and distance to the creek deserve extra attention. Bol Park anchors the area and Stanford is an easy bike ride, while shopping mostly means El Camino Real; comparable sales can be thin, and homes along El Camino face traffic noise.

South Palo Alto (Greenmeadow / Fairmeadow / Palo Verde)

Roughly East Meadow Drive to San Antonio Road, this is where Palo Alto's midcentury homes are most concentrated, including the National Register-listed Greenmeadow Historic District. Eichler-era houses call for an inspector who knows radiant heating in the slab and flat-roof waterproofing. It has the most direct drive toward Mountain View, Sunnyvale and southbound US-101, but rail grade-separation work at Meadow and Charleston and denser planning along San Antonio Road need a current status check.

Palo Alto Hills and the Foothills

The land west of Foothill Expressway, reached by Page Mill and Arastradero roads, holds large sloped lots with long driveways and homes of many ages and styles. Review slope drainage, road access, sewer connection and vegetation rules, and expect to drive downhill for most errands. Foothills Nature Preserve is close and I-280 is right there, but the state's 2025 maps place parts of this area in Moderate, High or Very High fire hazard zones, so ask about insurance before you offer.

Home types

Palo Alto is mainly single-family housing, and most of it has some age. Downtown, Professorville, Crescent Park and Old Palo Alto hold many homes from the first half of the 20th century, while Midtown, Barron Park and the south were built out after the war. Inside a FEMA special flood hazard area, new or substantially improved construction must sit above base flood elevation and effectively cannot include a basement.

Single-family homes

The core of the housing stock. Expect older homes and rebuilds on larger lots in the north, postwar homes and Eichlers in Midtown and the south, and irregular lots in Barron Park and the hills.

Townhomes

Fairly few, mostly in newer projects along El Camino Real and San Antonio Road and near California Avenue. Review HOA dues and reserves, guest parking, stairs and distance to rail or major roads.

Condos

Concentrated around Downtown, California Avenue, El Camino Real and San Antonio Road, in buildings from decades old to recent.

Transit and commuting

Driving depends on US-101 along the eastern edge, I-280 at the base of the hills, and El Camino Real through the middle, which works better for short trips because of its signals.

Caltrain stops at Palo Alto, which gets Express, Limited and Local trains, and California Avenue, which gets Local and Limited trains only; many south-side residents use San Antonio station in Mountain View. The city announced that its on-demand Palo Alto Link would end on October 30, 2026, so do not build a long-term plan around it.

Schools and official lookups

Palo Alto Unified School District serves TK through 12, but its boundary does not match the city limit. PAUSD says some Palo Alto addresses are outside the district, while parts of Los Altos Hills and Portola Valley are inside it. Start by entering the full street address in the district's official School Finder.

A Finder result shows the neighborhood school for an address, not a guaranteed seat. PAUSD notes that if a school is full, a student may be placed at another school in the same cluster and put on a waitlist, and Ohlone, Hoover and the Spanish Dual Immersion program at Escondido are lottery-based instead of tied to an address.

No address is guaranteed a particular school. Verify the full address and enrollment through the district’s official channels.

Daily life

Shopping is spread across small centers instead of one big hub: Whole Foods and University Avenue downtown, Town & Country Village at Embarcadero and El Camino, Safeway on Middlefield Road, California Avenue as a second walkable street, and Stanford Shopping Center for department stores and brands.

Baylands Nature Preserve offers about 15 miles of multi-use trails, and Foothills Nature Preserve in the hills charges a daily vehicle fee. Medical care centers on the Stanford campus on Welch Road, including Stanford Health Care's adult emergency department and Lucile Packard Children's Hospital. Check your insurance network before assuming the nearest hospital works for you, and drive the route once at rush hour.

Environment checks

Flooding is the first thing to check. Special flood hazard areas here come mainly from bay tides and San Francisquito Creek, so use FEMA's map by address along with the city's floodplain page. For earthquake hazards, look up the address in the California Geological Survey's EQ Zapp to see whether it is in a mapped fault rupture, liquefaction or landslide zone. For wildfire, check the CAL FIRE/OSFM hazard severity maps, since parts of the area west of Foothill Expressway are mapped in Moderate to Very High zones. These maps tell you what to look into further; they do not by themselves make a home safe or unsafe.
