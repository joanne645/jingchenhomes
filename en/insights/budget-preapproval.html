Home Buying Budget and Mortgage Preapproval

What a lender will approve and what feels comfortable are different numbers. Start with the second, then compare real quotes.

Reviewed Nov 5, 2026 · By Jing Chen

General information only, not lending advice or a loan commitment. Rates, amounts and terms are set by lenders; please consult a lending professional.

A budget answers two questions

How much will a lender let you borrow, and how much do you want to pay each month? Those are often different numbers, and the second one is your real budget. The CFPB's home loan toolkit offers a rough guide: keep your total monthly housing payment, including principal, interest, mortgage insurance, property tax, homeowners insurance and HOA dues, at or below 28% of pre-tax income. It is only a rule of thumb, and your savings and other debts matter too.

Count the whole monthly cost

  • Principal and interest: take this from a lender's quote.
  • Property tax: the base rate is 1% of taxable value, plus voter-approved debt and any special taxes such as Mello-Roos. Do not reuse the seller's bill, because the home is reassessed when it sells.
  • Homeowners insurance: get a quote before you offer, especially in a mapped fire hazard zone. Earthquake coverage is usually bought separately, so ask about it separately.
  • PMI: lenders usually require it when you put down less than 20%.
  • HOA and upkeep: include dues and set money aside for repairs.

The buyer cost calculator gives you a first pass at cash and monthly numbers. Replace its estimates with real quotes as you get them.

What a preapproval is

Prequalification and preapproval letters both show roughly what a lender may lend. The CFPB is clear that neither is a guaranteed offer, and lenders use the two terms inconsistently. Commonly, a prequalification rests on what you report and a preapproval rests on verified income, assets and credit. Ignore the label and ask what the lender actually verified.

Expect to provide income records, tax documents, bank and investment statements and debt details. If part of your down payment is a gift, document the amount and timing. Lenders generally want a signed gift letter.

How to compare lenders

  1. Get at least three written offers, as the CFPB recommends.
  2. Once you give a lender your name, income, Social Security number, property address, estimated value and loan amount, it must send a Loan Estimate within three business days.
  3. Ask each lender for the same loan type and features. Compare Total Loan Costs on page 2 and APR on page 3.
  4. Check whether the rate is locked. An unlocked rate can change.

According to the CFPB, mortgage credit checks within a 45-day window count as one inquiry, so comparing lenders costs you nothing extra there. A Loan Estimate is neither an approval nor a commitment on your part.

Conforming versus jumbo

FHFA's 2026 limits, announced November 25, 2025, are $832,750 for a one-unit home in most areas and $1,249,125 at the high-cost ceiling. FHFA's county list shows $1,249,125 for Santa Clara, Alameda, Contra Costa and San Mateo counties. As of November 5, 2026, we had not seen 2027 limits published. They change yearly, so confirm on FHFA's site. Loans above the limit are generally called jumbo, and their requirements and pricing vary by lender.

Common mistakes

  • Relying on a letter that ignores the actual taxes and HOA dues of the home you want.
  • Comparing rates without comparing fees and points.
  • Putting every dollar into the down payment, leaving nothing for repairs.
  • Taking on debt or moving large sums after preapproval without telling the lender.
This is general information, not loan advice. Terms depend on your situation. Jing is a real estate agent, not a loan officer, and does not recommend particular lenders.

Next step

Decide on a monthly number you can accept, work backward to a price range, and then request Loan Estimates from several lenders. Serious touring goes better with a letter in hand.
