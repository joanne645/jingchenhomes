A Buyer's Guide to Home Inspections

An inspection shows what the seller may not know. Book it right after acceptance and decide before the deadline.

Reviewed Nov 5, 2026 · By Jing Chen

General information only, not legal advice. Your rights and duties depend on your contract and applicable law; please consult an attorney.

What an inspection is

During your contingency period, you hire professionals to examine the home's condition. The CFPB puts the distinction plainly: a home inspector works for you and assesses condition, while an appraiser works for the lender and assesses value. One does not replace the other.

Seller disclosures tell you what the seller knows. An inspection can reveal what the seller doesn't. The C.A.R. RPA form's investigation contingency defaults to 17 days after acceptance, and that number is negotiable.

What usually gets checked

InspectionFocusWhen
General homeRoof, visible structure, plumbing, electrical, HVAC, doors and windowsWorth doing on nearly every home
Wood-destroying pestsTermites, dry rot and conditions that lead to themUsually
Roof, chimneyRemaining life, leaks, flue conditionWhen the general report points there
Foundation, drainageCracks, settlement, standing water, retaining wallsOlder homes, hillsides
ElectricalPanel capacity, wiring typeOlder homes, planned EV charging or solar
Sewer lineCamera check for breaks and rootsOlder homes, large trees
Pool, septic, wellEquipment and system conditionWhen present

Since January 1, 2026, Civil Code §1102.6i has required sellers to give buyers a statement that an electrical inspection by a qualified professional may be advisable, noting possible fire risk, insurance trouble and capacity limits. Buildings within three years of their certificate of occupancy are excluded.

How to run it

  1. Read existing reports before offering. Bay Area sellers often inspect in advance and include reports in the disclosure package. They help, but they are no substitute for your own judgment.
  2. Book right after acceptance. Specialists need lead time.
  3. Attend if you can. Hearing the inspector explain a finding beats reading it.
  4. Sort the findings into safety issues, major repairs and routine upkeep.
  5. Get numbers. Ask licensed contractors to price anything significant.
  6. Decide before the deadline: proceed, ask for repairs or a credit, or cancel as the contract allows.

Choosing an inspector

California has no dedicated home inspector license. Chapter 9.3 of the Business and Professions Code sets conduct rules and requires the care of a reasonably prudent inspector. Ask about credentials, insurance, a sample report and exactly what is covered. Pest inspections come from companies regulated by the Structural Pest Control Board.

Pest reports generally separate Section 1 items, meaning active infestation or damage, from Section 2 items, meaning conditions likely to lead to problems. Who pays for repairs depends on your contract.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming everything was checked. A general inspection typically covers what is visible and accessible. It doesn't open walls or certify code compliance.
  • Skimming past "further evaluation recommended." That phrase means the question is still open.
  • Not checking permits. Confirm remodel permits with the city or county building department.
  • Waiving the contingency and skipping inspections. You are then buying with unknowns, and your deposit may be exposed.
  • Forgetting insurance. Roof or wiring issues can affect coverage, so get quotes as soon as findings arrive.
This is general information. Your signed contract sets your deadlines and rights, so take legal questions to an attorney. Jing is not an inspector, and technical calls belong to qualified professionals.

Next step

Read the existing reports before you offer, list what else you want checked, and set aside time and money. When results arrive, weigh them with the disclosures and your contract terms.
