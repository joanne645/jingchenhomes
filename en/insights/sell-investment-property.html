Selling an Investment Property: Depreciation, Gains and CA Withholding

An investment sale involves more moving parts than selling your home: depreciation, federal and state tax, and withholding at closing. These are the concepts to discuss with your CPA.

Reviewed Nov 5, 2026 · By Jing Chen

General information only, not tax or legal advice. Please consult a CPA, tax professional or attorney about your situation.

Why run the numbers early

Your main home may qualify for the $250,000/$500,000 gain exclusion. A pure investment property generally does not. What you net depends on how long you held it, the depreciation taken, your income and what you do next, and some choices, like a 1031 exchange, have to be set up before closing. Ask a CPA for an estimate before you list. The rates below are general concepts, not a calculation for your situation.

Working out the gain

In rough terms, gain is net sale price minus adjusted basis. Adjusted basis starts with your purchase cost, adds capitalized improvements and subtracts depreciation. Residential rental buildings depreciate over 27.5 years, and land does not depreciate.

One trap: the IRS reduces basis by depreciation you could have claimed, even if you never did. If your records are patchy, get a CPA to review them first.

How federal and California tax apply

ItemGeneral rule (checked November 2026)
DepreciationUnrecaptured section 1250 gain on the sale is taxed federally at up to 25%
Long-term capital gainHeld more than one year: 0%, 15% or 20% federally. Income thresholds adjust each year, so check the IRS figures for the year of sale.
Net investment income taxA possible 3.8% when modified AGI exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (joint), with exceptions for certain nonpassive businesses and qualifying real estate professionals
California income taxThe Franchise Tax Board (FTB) states California has no lower capital gains rate; gains are taxed as ordinary income

The sale is generally reported on Form 4797. Ask your CPA whether any suspended passive losses are released on the sale.

California withholding and Form 593

On a sale of California real estate above $100,000, investment properties usually face withholding at closing. The escrow company or other real estate escrow person handles this and remits to the FTB by the 20th of the month after closing.

  • Default method: 3⅓% of the sale price. A $1 million sale means about $33,300, based on price rather than profit.
  • Alternative method: estimated gain multiplied by the maximum rate (12.3% for individuals and trusts in the 2026 instructions). You must sign to elect it.
  • Exemptions: a loss or zero gain, with the calculation shown, and some 1031 exchanges, among others. The principal residence exemption does not cover rentals, second homes or vacant land.

Form 593 must reach escrow before closing, or withholding applies. It is a prepayment, claimed on your California return. Any overpayment comes back only after the FTB processes that return.

Special situations

  • Former home now rented: if you meet the two-of-five-year test you may still exclude gain, but not the part equal to depreciation, and periods of nonqualified use after 2008 can reduce the exclusion.
  • Installment sale: withholding applies to the down payment, and the buyer then withholds on the principal portion of later payments.
  • Deferral: a 1031 exchange must be arranged before closing. It delays tax rather than erasing it.
  • Foreign sellers: the buyer may need to withhold federal tax, so raise this with a CPA early.
Common mistakes: looking only at price minus loan balance, losing improvement receipts, forgetting depreciation, thinking of a 1031 days before closing, and treating 3⅓% as the full tax bill.

Next step

Gather your purchase closing statement, improvement invoices and depreciation schedules, and have a CPA prepare a sale estimate. If tenants are in place, read selling a rental property and the rental rules as well, because a notice error can delay delivery of the property.
