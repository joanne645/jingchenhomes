The California First-Time Buyer Process

What happens at each stage of a first Bay Area purchase, and why the contingency period needs the most planning.

Reviewed Nov 5, 2026 · By Jing Chen

General information only, not legal advice. Your rights and duties depend on your contract and applicable law; please consult an attorney.

The shape of the process

Buying in California breaks into three stretches: preparing before you offer, the contingency period after your offer is accepted, and escrow through recording. First-time buyers tend to underestimate the middle one. It is short, and inspections, disclosures, the loan and insurance all run at once, each with its own deadline.

Three things to know before you start

Expect a written agreement with your agent

Since August 17, 2024, an MLS participant working with a buyer has needed a written agreement before touring a home, a result of the NAR settlement. California added its own rules in Civil Code §1670.50: a buyer agreement can run no longer than three months and cannot renew automatically. DRE regulations require it to be signed no later than your offer and presume it is practical to sign before any showing, in person or virtual. The C.A.R. form is the Buyer Representation and Broker Compensation Agreement (BRBC).

Before signing, read four items: the term, whether it is exclusive, the compensation amount and who pays it, and how the agreement can end early. Commissions are negotiable and not set by law. You can check any agent's license on the DRE lookup.

Plan for three separate pots of cash

You need the down payment, the deposit and closing costs. The deposit counts toward your purchase but has to arrive soon after acceptance. DRE's homebuying checklist gives typical ranges of 1%–3% of the price for the deposit and 3%–7% for closing costs. Your actual numbers come from your own quotes and your Closing Disclosure.

Check what "first-time buyer" means for each program

CalHFA, for example, counts you as first-time if you have not owned and occupied a home in the last three years, with some exceptions. Its MyHome program, as shown on the agency's page in November 2026, offers deferred-payment help with the down payment and closing costs. It has income limits, runs through approved lenders and requires a homebuyer education course. Confirm eligibility before building your budget around any assistance.

Where first-timers get caught

  • Treating the approved amount as the budget. What a lender will approve and what feels comfortable each month can be very different numbers.
  • Changing finances after preapproval. A new job, a new credit card or a car loan can disrupt underwriting. DRE's checklist makes the same point.
  • Treating dates as suggestions. Every day count in the contract has consequences. Keep a list of deadlines and work ahead of them.
  • Forgetting the tax bill after closing. The home is reassessed when ownership changes, and a supplemental bill usually follows. See the buyer FAQ.
This is general information. Your signed documents control, and legal, tax or loan questions belong with an attorney, CPA or loan professional.

Next step

Write down three things: the monthly cost you can live with, the cash you can actually use, and the places you need to reach each week. With those in hand you can talk to a loan professional about preapproval, and work through cities and timing with Jing.
