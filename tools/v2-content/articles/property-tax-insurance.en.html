How Prop 13 sets your bill

California does not tax your home on this year's market value. Per the Board of Equalization (BOE), the rate is "1 percent plus" amounts for voter-approved debt, applied to an assessed value set when you bought or built. That value can rise by no more than 2% a year for inflation. A change in ownership or completed new construction triggers reassessment at market value, which is why two similar houses on one street can carry very different bills.

Your bill may also list direct charges that are not based on the 1%, such as parcel taxes or Mello-Roos. See holding costs for how those fit into the bigger picture.

Things you have to do yourself

  • Homeowners' Exemption. It takes $7,000 off the assessed value of an owner-occupied main home (as of November 2026), about $70 a year at 1%. It is not automatic. File form BOE-266 once with the county assessor, by February 15 to get the full year.
  • Supplemental tax. After a purchase or completed construction, the county bills the difference between the old and new value, prorated for the rest of the fiscal year. Events between January 1 and May 31 produce two supplemental assessments. Ask whether your impound account covers these rather than assuming it does.
  • Due dates. The first installment is due November 1 and delinquent after December 10. The second is due February 1 and delinquent after April 10. The late penalty is 10%.
  • Decline in value. If market value falls below assessed value, you can ask for a Prop 8 review. Deadlines differ. Santa Clara's informal cutoff for the 2026/27 roll was August 8, 2026. Formal 2026 appeals closed September 15 in Alameda and Santa Clara and run through November 30 in Contra Costa. Check your county each year.

Prop 19 in brief

Owners who are 55 or older, severely disabled, or disaster victims can move their taxable value to a replacement home anywhere in California if they buy or build within two years of selling. Those 55 or older or disabled can do this up to three times. A pricier replacement adds the excess to the transferred value; move-up planning walks through the math.

Parent-to-child transfers are narrower. The child must make it their main home within a year, and the excluded value is capped at the existing taxable value plus $1,044,586 for transfers from February 16, 2025 through February 15, 2027. The figure next adjusts in February 2027. Talk to an estate attorney and CPA before transferring anything.

Reading your insurance policy

The California Department of Insurance says your dwelling limit should reflect rebuilding cost, not market value, since land generally is not insured. At each renewal, check whether you have replacement cost coverage, whether ordinance or law coverage pays for code upgrades, and whether loss of use is adequate. Condo owners should separate what the HOA master policy covers from their unit policy, and review loss assessment coverage.

The FAIR Plan is a fallback for people who cannot get regular coverage. As of November 2026, the Department lists a residential limit of $3 million per location. It remains a limited fire policy, so water damage or liability usually needs a separate Difference in Conditions policy.

Earthquake coverage is excluded from standard policies, although fire following a quake must be covered by law. Your insurer must offer earthquake coverage every other year, and silence for 30 days counts as declining. California Earthquake Authority (CEA) policies are sold only through participating insurers, with deductibles of 5% to 25% of the Coverage A and B limit. As of November 2026, the 5% and 10% options are off the table if Coverage A exceeds $1 million, or for pre-1980 frame homes not on a slab without a verified retrofit. The arithmetic matters: a 15% deductible on a $1 million limit is $150,000.

Common mistakes: forgetting BOE-266 after closing, tossing a supplemental bill as junk mail, insuring to the purchase price, and comparing premiums without comparing deductibles and exclusions.
