Start by working out which rules apply

California's Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482) covers most rental housing, and city ordinances can go further. Where both apply, you generally have to satisfy both. Before acting, confirm the building's age, who owns it (an individual or a company), and which city it is in.

Rent increase limits

The cap is 5% plus regional CPI, never more than 10%, across any 12-month period. These figures come from the California Attorney General's site as of November 2026:

RegionIncreases starting 8/1/25–7/31/26Increases starting 8/1/26–7/31/27
San Francisco area counties, including Alameda and Contra Costa6.3%8.8%
All other counties, including Santa Clara7.7%8.6%

Give at least 30 days' formal written notice for increases up to 10% and 90 days for more. A call, text or email does not count.

Main exemptions: housing built within the last 15 years; single-family homes and condos not owned by a REIT, corporation or LLC with a corporate member, if the tenant received written notice (for tenancies from July 1, 2020 it must be in the lease, or the exemption is lost); and owner-occupied duplexes.

Just cause

Once all tenants have lived in the unit for 12 months, or any one for 24 months, you need a legal reason to end the tenancy. A lease expiring is not one. Reasons divide into at-fault (nonpayment, breach, illegal activity) and no-fault (owner or family move-in, withdrawing from the market, major repairs or demolition). No-fault terminations require one month's rent in relocation assistance or a waiver of the last month's rent. Notice errors can lose the case, so this is a good place to use an attorney. AB 1482 is set to expire January 1, 2030.

Security deposits

  • Since July 1, 2024 the limit is generally one month's rent. Individuals, or LLCs made up only of individuals, with no more than two rental properties and four units in total may charge two months. This exception does not apply to tenants on active military duty.
  • Return the balance with an itemized statement within 21 days of move-out. If repair and cleaning deductions total $125 or less, receipts need not be attached, but the statement is still required.
  • Tenancies starting on or after July 1, 2025 require photos before or at move-in. Since April 1, 2025, landlords must also photograph after move-out, before repairs or cleaning, and again afterward, and supply the photos with the statement.
  • New for 2026: deposits received digitally generally go back digitally, and leases signed, amended or renewed from January 1, 2026 generally require a working stove and refrigerator. The landlord must supply the stove. A tenant may voluntarily supply the refrigerator if the lease says so, but it cannot be a condition of renting. Some housing types are exempt.

Local rules as of November 2026

  • San Jose: The Apartment Rent Ordinance covers buildings of three or more units with occupancy certificates on or before September 7, 1979, limiting increases to 5% in 12 months and requiring annual registry updates. The Tenant Protection Ordinance requires just cause in buildings of three or more units, with notices copied to the Housing Department within three days.
  • Mountain View: The CSFRA covers pre-1995 multifamily rentals fully and buildings first occupied from 1995 through December 23, 2016 for just cause only; later buildings are exempt. Single-family homes, condos, duplexes and companion units are exempt. The committee publishes the annual adjustment on the city's site.
  • Palo Alto: Register rental units. The one-year lease offer rule excludes single-family homes and condos. Relocation assistance varies by unit count; see PAMC 9.68 and confirm with the city.
  • Milpitas: Increases above 5% can go to a Rent Review Board with non-binding findings, and one increase is allowed per 12 months, with some exemptions.
  • Fremont: The Rent Review Program asks tenants to call within 15 days of receiving a notice.
  • Sunnyvale and Cupertino: The cities point to Project Sentinel for dispute mediation.
  • Dublin, Pleasanton and San Ramon: None appears on the Attorney General's local cap list, but confirm any other requirements with the city.
Common mistakes: assuming a state exemption also clears city rules, leaving exemption language out of a single-family lease, deducting from a deposit without photos, and ending a tenancy with a generic template. Fair housing law applies to tenant screening too.

Next step

Identify which category your property falls into, then update your lease template. Take specific disputes to a landlord-tenant attorney and questions on rental income, depreciation and repairs to a CPA.
