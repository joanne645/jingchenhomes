A year of upkeep in the Bay Area

With a concentrated rainy season and long dry months, the rhythm here differs from colder parts of the country. Treat this as a general guide and adjust for your home's age, roof and foundation.

  • Fall, before the rain: clear gutters and downspouts, check the roof and window seals, confirm water drains away from the foundation, and service the furnace.
  • Winter: after heavy storms, look for moisture on ceilings, in the crawl space and in below-grade garages. Early detection is far cheaper than opening walls.
  • Spring: test irrigation for leaks, clear dead brush from around the house, and schedule a pest inspection.
  • Summer: service the air conditioning, check decks, fences and exterior paint, and use the dry weather for outside repairs.
  • Anytime: test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, learn where the gas and water shutoffs are, and confirm the water heater is secured.

State law (Health and Safety Code 19211) requires residential water heaters to be braced, and sellers must certify this in writing. Owners of pre-1980 raised-foundation houses should look at Earthquake Brace + Bolt, which has offered up to $3,000 in designated ZIP codes in past rounds. Check the program site for current registration dates and eligibility.

Ask about permits first

Your city's building division decides what needs a permit, and thresholds vary. Structural changes, electrical, plumbing or gas work, additions and resized windows are all worth asking about before work starts. The Contractors State License Board (CSLB) advises that your contractor should pull permits and arrange inspections. Name the responsible party in the contract.

Skipping permits tends to cost you later. The California Transfer Disclosure Statement asks sellers directly whether they know of additions, alterations or repairs made without necessary permits or out of compliance with code, and buyers may renegotiate on that basis.

On property tax, the BOE says normal maintenance and repairs are typically not assessed. With an addition, only the new portion is reassessed. Seismic retrofits can be excluded, but you must notify the assessor within 30 days of completion and submit documents within six months.

Checking a contractor

  1. As of November 2026, work of $1,000 or more in combined labor and materials requires a CSLB license, as does any project needing a permit or involving workers.
  2. Look up the license at cslb.ca.gov or call 800-321-2752. Confirm it is in good standing, covers the type of work, and shows workers' compensation coverage where required.
  3. Match their photo ID to the pocket license, get at least three bids, and call references.

Contracts and payments

  • The down payment is limited to 10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less, unless the contractor has a qualifying blanket bond on file with CSLB.
  • Keep progress payments behind the work completed. Hold the final payment until you are satisfied, and avoid cash.
  • Contracts signed in your home generally carry a three-business-day right to cancel, or five if you are 65 or older. This does not apply to contracts negotiated at the contractor's place of business or to emergency repairs.
  • Put change orders in writing with both signatures. The contract should cover scope, materials, price, payment schedule, dates and who handles permits.
Mechanics liens catch many owners off guard. An unpaid subcontractor or supplier can file a lien against your home even if you paid the general contractor in full. They typically send a Preliminary Notice within 20 days of starting work or delivering materials. Keep those notices, collect lien releases as you pay, or use joint checks.

Next step

Keep maintenance records, permits, invoices and warranties in one folder. They help with insurance claims and with disclosure when you sell. For a contract dispute or a lien, speak with an attorney; complaints against licensees can go to CSLB.
