ADU versus JADU

An accessory dwelling unit is a separate living unit on the same lot with its own kitchen and bath. It can be detached, attached, or converted from a garage or other space. A junior ADU is smaller: up to 500 square feet of interior livable space, built within a single-family home (including an attached garage), limited to one per lot, with at least an efficiency kitchen and either a private or shared bathroom.

The state baseline

These points come from the Department of Housing and Community Development's (HCD) March 2026 ADU Handbook and are current as of November 2026. Cities can be more generous, but your city still reviews whether a given project qualifies.

Living there and renting it out

Cities cannot require owner occupancy for ADUs, apart from units sold separately to qualified buyers. Since January 2026, a JADU requires owner occupancy only if it shares a bathroom with the main home, and JADUs cannot be short-term rentals. JADUs and ADUs approved under Government Code section 66323 must be rented for terms longer than 30 days.

ADUs generally cannot be sold apart from the main home. State law lets a city opt in to allowing condominium-style separate sales, so check whether yours has done so.

How to approach a project

  1. Start with your city's ADU page for zoning, size, setback and fee rules. Many cities offer pre-approved plans.
  2. Check the lot itself: title restrictions, easements, sewer and electrical capacity, and CC&Rs if you are in an HOA.
  3. Get bids from licensed professionals covering design, permits, utility connections and construction, not only the build.
  4. Ask a CPA and an insurance professional how rent and depreciation are treated and whether your policy covers the new unit. The BOE reassesses the newly built portion; the existing home keeps its value.
  5. If you need financing, talk to a loan professional early about draw timing and repayment.
