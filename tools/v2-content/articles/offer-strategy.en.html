An offer is more than a price

Sellers weigh the whole contract: price, financing certainty, contingencies, deposit, timing and any credits requested. The classic mistake in competition is giving up protection against a risk you haven't sized. A good offer is one you could live with if things went wrong.

The RPA's default timing

Most California home sales use C.A.R.'s California Residential Purchase Agreement (RPA). These are the form's printed defaults. All are negotiable, and whatever is filled in on your contract controls.

ItemDefault
Initial depositTo escrow within 3 business days of acceptance
Seller delivers documentsWithin 7 days of acceptance
Loan, appraisal, investigation contingencies17 days after acceptance each
Review of seller documents, preliminary report, HOA documents17 days after acceptance or 5 days after receipt, whichever is later
Final verification of condition5 days before closing
The RPA is revised from time to time. These are common printed defaults, so rely on the version you actually sign.

Deadlines don't remove contingencies by themselves. You must remove them in writing, typically on a Contingency Removal form, or cancel. If a deadline passes, the seller can serve a Notice to Buyer to Perform giving at least two days, and only then may cancel.

Weighing contingencies

ContingencyWhat it protectsShorten or waive only if
InvestigationExit or renegotiate if problems appearYou've read the disclosures and reports, ideally with your own inspector
LoanThe loan fails to come throughYour lender has reviewed the file and you can accept the outcome if it fails
AppraisalThe appraisal comes in below priceYou have the cash to cover the gap
InsuranceAcceptable coverage isn't availableYou already hold a real quote
Sale of your homeYour current home doesn't sellYou can close without that sale

C.A.R. added insurance contingency terms in 2024. Its June 2026 revisions state that the loan contingency cannot be used when a buyer can't obtain insurance, so handle them separately. The same revisions added an option based on an appraisal gap, which lets you agree to cover a set shortfall instead of choosing all or nothing. The form itself notes that removing contingencies at offer is against agent advice, so be sure you understand what you are giving up.

Deposit and default risk

DRE's checklist puts deposits at about 1%–3% of the price. Walking away after removing contingencies can put yours at risk. For a home of up to four units the buyer intends to occupy, Civil Code §1675 presumes liquidated damages valid up to 3% of the price, to the extent actually paid. Above that, the party relying on the clause must show it is reasonable. Whether the clause applies depends on whether it was initialed, so ask an attorney.

How to prepare

  1. Review comparable sales and set your limit before you negotiate.
  2. Finish the disclosures and get an insurance quote.
  3. Have your lender confirm the preapproval fits this property.
  4. Find out what the seller values, such as timing, a rent-back or certainty. Those can cost you little.
  5. Decide how buyer-broker compensation is handled. You can ask the seller to pay it in your offer, but the seller need not agree, and it can't exceed your buyer agreement.

Common mistakes

  • Bidding on emotion. Go back to the limit you set earlier.
  • Skimming a counter offer. SCO and SMCO forms can change days, costs and terms.
  • Waiving protection without the cash behind it.
  • Checking the loan and insurance days before a deadline.
This is general information, not legal or loan advice. Before shortening or waiving a contingency, ask an attorney and a loan professional what it means for you.
