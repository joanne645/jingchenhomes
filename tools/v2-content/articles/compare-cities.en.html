What you are really comparing

Differences between Bay Area cities only mean something once they land on a specific home. A city name gives you a general location. Within one city, school districts, tax bills, noise and commutes can change within a few blocks, so settle on a method before you settle on a conclusion.

A four-step comparison

  1. List your fixed points. Workplace, days in the office, anywhere else you must reach regularly.
  2. Pick two or three candidate areas, not ten cities, and choose one representative address in each.
  3. Drive it at real times. Try morning and evening peaks, and count transfers and parking.
  4. Score them on one sheet, so impressions don't decide for you.

Costs that are easy to miss

California's base property tax is 1%, but the local bonds layered on top differ by address. Newer communities may add Mello-Roos or CFD special taxes, and those amounts can vary between phases of the same development. Two homes at the same price can have noticeably different monthly costs, so read the tax bill instead of estimating from a rate.

Insurance belongs in the comparison as well. Get quotes early for homes near hillsides or open space. CAL FIRE released new Local Responsibility Area zone maps in phases during February and March 2025, so older information may be out of date. The zones describe long-term hazard from conditions like terrain and vegetation, not a forecast of damage to one house. Ask your local jurisdiction about a specific property.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing median prices alone. Home types, sizes and ages differ, so the medians aren't like for like.
  • Judging from a weekend visit. Weekday traffic and noise can be a different story.
  • Going by reputation. Forum talk about "good areas" is mostly opinion, and agents cannot describe neighborhoods by race, religion or family status. Compare on things you can verify.
  • Betting on resale. Nobody can promise future prices. Make sure the home fits your life and finances today.
