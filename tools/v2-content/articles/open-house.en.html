What it does

An open house lets buyers walk through at a set time without an appointment. It reaches nearby buyers and people who haven't hired an agent yet, and lets them compare homes in person. Not every home needs one. It depends on location, condition, whether someone lives there and how you feel about privacy. Showings by appointment may be enough.

Getting ready

  • NAR suggests holding the first one soon after listing, ideally the first weekend.
  • Clean, declutter, set the temperature and lighting, and open the curtains.
  • Offer a flyer with details only an owner knows, such as utility costs.
  • Plan for shoes, pets, parking and neighbors.
  • Provide disclosures through the proper channel and keep personal documents off the table.

Protecting your home

NAR's advice for homeowners is simple to follow:

  • Remove or hide valuables, prescription drugs and sensitive papers such as checkbooks, and take pets elsewhere.
  • Don't share garage or door codes. Use a separate showing code and change it regularly.
  • Photograph each room beforehand so missing or moved items are easier to spot.

Also check that doors and windows are locked, including the back gate and windows you rarely use, and walk through the house again afterward.

Safety for the host

NAR recommends working with a colleague, keeping visitors in front of you, staying out of attics and crawl spaces, limiting how many people are inside, and leaving together, since the end of the event can be the riskiest time. These are fair points to raise when you discuss showing plans with your agent.

Cameras and audio

Many homes have doorbell and indoor cameras. California Penal Code §632 prohibits intentionally recording a confidential conversation without the consent of all parties. Whether buyers quietly discussing a price are in a confidential conversation depends on the facts, but the risk is real. The cautious approach is to turn off audio recording or post a visible notice, and to ask an attorney about your setup. Don't try to listen in on buyers to shape your negotiating strategy.

Tenant-occupied homes

If a tenant lives there, an open house doesn't give you open-ended access. Civil Code §1954 allows entry for specific purposes such as showing the home to buyers, generally during normal business hours and with advance notice, and bars abuse of that right. See selling a rental property. If the tenant objects, stick to scheduled showings and speak with an attorney.

Common mistakes

  • Relying on the open house as the only marketing step.
  • A sign-in process that doesn't really work.
  • Sizing up visitors by appearance or accent. Fair housing rules require treating everyone equally.
  • Sellers staying to chat and giving away their bottom line or moving pressure.

Also, per NAR, a visitor who only asks an agent about services at an open house doesn't need to sign a buyer agreement first.

Next step
