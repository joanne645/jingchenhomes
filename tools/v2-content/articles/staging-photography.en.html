What staging is

Staging means furnishing a home for sale so buyers can read its size and use. There are three common approaches:

ApproachWhen it fitsWatch for
Edit what you ownGood furniture, just too much of itInexpensive, but a lot has to leave the house
Partial or full stagingVacant homes, or furniture that works against the roomsFee and rental term, usually through a staging company; a longer listing may mean an extension
Virtual stagingVacant homes, online images onlyCalifornia disclosure rules apply, and the home looks different in person

Whether any of this makes sense depends on the home, the price point and the competition. Confirm cost and terms with the provider.

Getting ready for photos

  • Finish repairs, cleaning and furnishing first so you don't have to reshoot.
  • Clear counters, family photos, medications and anything with personal information.
  • Turn on every light, wash the windows, trim the front yard.
  • Move cars and trash bins out of view.
  • Think about timing. Light is best at different hours depending on which way the home faces.

Floor plans and video help out-of-area buyers follow the layout; whether you need them depends on the home. Aerial shots suit properties where the lot or setting matters. Commercial drone flights fall under FAA Part 107, which generally calls for a Remote Pilot Certificate and compliance with airspace rules, so ask before you hire someone.

California's rule on altered images

Under Business and Professions Code §10140.8 (AB 723, in effect as of November 2026), an image is digitally altered if an agent, or someone working for the agent, uses editing software or AI to add, remove or change elements such as furniture, flooring, paint color, landscaping, the facade, or things visible from the property like views, utility poles and neighboring homes. When such an image appears in advertising for a sale, it must:

  • carry a reasonably conspicuous statement, on or next to the image, that it was altered;
  • provide a link, URL or QR code to the original image;
  • on sites the agent controls, also show the original or link to it.

Routine edits such as lighting, sharpening, white balance, color correction, angle, cropping and exposure don't count, as long as they don't change how the property is represented. Virtual furniture, a greener lawn or a new wall color would. If you aren't sure, ask the MLS or an attorney.

Common mistakes

  • Lens choices that make rooms look larger than they are.
  • Editing out power lines or a neighbor's house, which misleads buyers and may trigger the disclosure rule.
  • Furniture that blocks doors, windows or a condition that should be disclosed.
  • Reusing old photos after the seasons or the home have changed.
